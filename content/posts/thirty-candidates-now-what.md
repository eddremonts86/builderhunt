There is a moment in every search when the shortlist
stops being a list of interesting names and starts being
a pile of decisions you have not made. You have 30
candidates. You cannot interview 30. You cannot
outreach 30. The list is the input; the work is the
output. The transition is the part nobody writes
guides for, because it is not a search-engine problem
and not a tool problem. It is a judgement problem, and
the only honest guide is the one that respects the
judgement you have to make.

This post is about the moment. The shape of the
shortlist, the order to read it in, the specific
questions to ask each row, and the specific ways to
retire candidates without losing the work you did to
find them.

## The state you are in

You have 30 tracked candidates. You have notes on
maybe half of them. You have 1-3 saved searches
running. You have not sent any outreach yet, or you
have sent five and gotten two replies. The radar
inbox is full but the full inbox is not the problem.
The problem is the 30. The list is the ceiling on
your judgement until you decide who is in and who
is out.

The natural reaction is to triage — open each row,
decide quickly, move on. The natural reaction is
also wrong, for the same reason it is wrong on
resumes: a fast triage decides based on the visible
signal, and the visible signal on a shortlist is
already the signal that got them into the
shortlist. The interesting decision is the one that
asks the question the shortlist did not.

## The order to read them in

Not alphabetical. Not by score. Not by recency. In
the order that maximises the amount of decision per
hour:

1. **First, the 3-5 strongest.** The candidates
   you have been meaning to read more of. The
   ones whose track action you hesitated over
   because the read was going to be long. Read
   them now, in depth. Read the code, read the
   posts, read the timeline. Form a view.
2. **Then, the 3-5 weakest.** The candidates
   you tracked because something about the row
   looked interesting at the time and that
   "something" has faded. Re-read the row.
   If the something is still there, the
   candidate is not weak. If it is not, the
   retire action is the right one, and the
   retire action is fast.
3. **Then, the 20-24 in the middle.** These are
   the candidates that are interesting but
   undifferentiated — they have all the
   signals, none of them stands out, and the
   list feels interchangeable. The middle is
   where the most productive time goes,
   because the middle is where the tie-break
   lives.

The order matters because the strong and the
weak give you calibration. A "strong" candidate
who turns out to be less strong than you
remember resets the bar. A "weak" candidate
who turns out to be more interesting than you
remember resets the other direction. The middle
is read with the bar in mind.

## The four questions for each candidate

For each candidate, in order, answer these four
questions. The questions are designed to produce
a decision, not a description. A candidate you
cannot answer four questions about is a
candidate you do not have enough information
about, and the right response to that is to
re-read the work, not to skip the question.

### 1. Could I write the first sentence of an outreach email right now?

The first sentence is the test. Not the whole
email — the first sentence, the line that
anchors on something the candidate has done or
said. If you can write it without reaching for
a generic phrase, the candidate is real to
you. If you cannot, the candidate is a name on
a list, and the right response is either to
read more of their work or to retire them.

A candidate you can write the first sentence
about is a candidate you understand well
enough to talk to. A candidate you cannot is
a candidate you do not, and the conversation
you would have with them is the conversation
you would have with a stranger.

### 2. What would I learn from a 30-minute call that I cannot learn from their public work?

A 30-minute call is expensive. The candidate
has to take time off, you have to take time
off, the call has to be worth more than the
sum of its alternatives. The question is not
"is the candidate interesting" — they all are,
they would not be on the list if they were
not. The question is "is there a question
that the public work cannot answer, that the
call can, that is decision-relevant".

A candidate whose work is fully legible is a
candidate you can decide about without
talking to them. A candidate whose work
raises a specific question — "the design
decision in this commit is interesting, can
you walk me through the trade-off you were
making?" — is a candidate the call would
help. The first kind does not need a slot;
the second kind does.

### 3. If they say no, does the role stay open or does the shortlist change shape?

This is the question most recruiters skip,
and it is the most important one. A "yes"
from the candidate advances the role. A "no"
from the candidate should also tell you
something — either the role is unattractive
or the candidate was not actually a fit, and
the two have different consequences.

A candidate whose "no" would change the
shape of your shortlist is a candidate
you are relying on. A candidate whose "no"
is one of several parallel paths is a
candidate you are not. The candidates you
are relying on are the ones whose call you
should book first, because their response
shapes the next move.

### 4. What is the one thing I would want to know about them after the call?

This is the question that turns the
shortlist from a list into a hiring
process. A candidate whose "one thing"
is "can they do the work" is a candidate
whose interview is a screening. A candidate
whose "one thing" is "do they want to do
this work" is a candidate whose interview
is a fit check. The "one thing" determines
the kind of interview you book.

The four answers together tell you the
action: send the outreach, retire the
candidate, or park them for a second look
in a week. The action is the output of
the shortlist, and the shortlist is the
input.

## What to do with the answers

Write them down. The notes panel on a
tracked candidate is the right place. Two
sentences per question is enough; the goal
is to capture the decision, not to
document the candidate. A note that says
"first sentence possible from the work on
distributed consensus, but the team-fit
question is open" is a more useful note
than a paragraph that summarises the
candidate's public work, which is the kind
of summary that goes stale in a week.

The notes are also what your team reads
when the role gets shared. A team that
inherits a shortlist with notes is a team
that can move on the shortlist without
re-doing the work. A team that inherits a
shortlist without notes is a team that
re-builds the shortlist from scratch, and
the rebuilding is the most expensive part
of the handover.

## How to retire candidates without losing the work

A retired candidate is not a deleted
candidate. A retired candidate is a
candidate whose record stays in the system
but whose row stops being part of the
active shortlist. The notes, the
enrichment, and the persona card all stay.
The decision to retire is reversible, and
the reversal is one click.

The reason retired is not deleted is the
same reason the saved searches are kept
even when they are not producing results:
the work to find the candidate was real,
and the work to evaluate the candidate was
real, and the next time the role is open
or a similar role is open, the notes are
still useful. A shortlist that forgets
itself every quarter is a shortlist that
re-pays the cost of finding candidates
every quarter.

The "retire" action also captures a
reason, and the reason is the
documentation. A shortlist that says
"retired: not a fit for the role" is a
shortlist that can be audited. A
shortlist that says "retired" is a
shortlist that has lost the
information.

## The cadence of the decision

The 30-candidate decision is not a
single-session event. It is a three-day
process: day one is the strong and the
weak, day two is the middle, day three
is the outreach batch. Spreading the
decision across three days is what
keeps the judgement honest; a single
day of triage produces a list of
decisions you would not have made on
day two.

The cadence is also what makes the
sourcing loop sustainable. A
shortlist that takes a week to
decide on is a shortlist that does
not get decided on; a shortlist that
takes a day is a shortlist that
produces bad decisions. Three days
is the design point.

## What the shortlist looks like at the end

After three days, the 30 is reduced to
maybe 8-12 active candidates, 5-8
retired with reasons, and the start of
the outreach batch. The retired
candidates are not lost; they are in
the system. The active candidates are
the ones whose outreach drafts are
written. The outreach batch is the
input to the next loop, and the next
loop is the loop that fills the role
or decides the role cannot be filled
through sourcing alone.

A shortlist that has been through the
three-day process is a shortlist you
have a relationship with. The notes
are yours, the decisions are yours, the
retired candidates are documented
yours. The role is not yet filled, and
the next shortlist will be a different
30, and the process is the same.

[Open your shortlist](/search) and run
the four-question pass on the 30. The
shortlist you have at the end of the
three-day process is the shortlist
that fills the role. The shortlist
you had at the start of the day is a
pile of names. The work is the
difference.
