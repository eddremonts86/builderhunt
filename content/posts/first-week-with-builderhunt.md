You signed up. You opened the search box. You typed "react
developer" and saw a wall of results that all look the same
and none of them answer the question you actually have, which
is "is this person a good fit for the role I am trying to
fill".

This is the week-by-week guide for the first five days. It
assumes one open role, one person doing the sourcing, and
the free tier of BuilderHunt. It also works on the paid tiers
with more headroom; the only difference is the daily limit on
how many searches you can run.

The plan is shaped around the work, not the product. The
product is the substrate; the work is the loop.

## Day 1 — search for the work, not the job title

Open the search box. Type what the person does, not what
their profile says they are. "postgres performance", not
"senior backend engineer". "tokio async runtime", not
"rust developer". The first form finds people who have
shipped and written about the work; the second form finds
people who wrote the phrase in a bio.

Run four or five of these searches. The point is to find
the queries that return interesting results, not to find
the right query on the first try. A search that returns
three good candidates and a hundred misses is more useful
than a search that returns a hundred results and three
good candidates, because the first one tells you
"this is the right query", and the second one tells you
"this is a noisy query, refine it".

For each good result, click through. Read the source the
candidate came from. Check the recency — when was the
last time they shipped something in the area you are
hiring for? A candidate whose last meaningful activity
in your area is two years old is a different candidate
than one whose last activity is last week, regardless
of how impressive the older work is.

By the end of day one you should have one or two
queries that return useful results, and a small set of
candidates (5-10) you want to look at again tomorrow.
Save the queries; do not save the candidates yet. A
candidate is not worth tracking until you have read at
least one piece of their work end to end.

## Day 2 — read the work, not the score

Open the candidates from day one. For each one, read
the most recent piece of public work in the area you
are hiring for. A merged PR. A post. A package release.
A Stack Overflow answer with depth. Twenty minutes per
candidate, maximum. The point is not to evaluate their
work in detail; the point is to decide whether the
candidate is the kind of person you would want to talk
to, or whether the score pulled them into your list
through sheer keyword coincidence.

Track the ones that pass. Use the track button. The
track action is the only thing that creates durable
state, and the only thing that lets you attach notes,
send an outreach draft, or run a team-fit analysis
later. The five-minute version of "I will look at
this again later" is to track them; the hour-long
version is to maintain a spreadsheet nobody else on
your team can read.

The ones that do not pass, leave alone. A candidate
who is technically qualified but the wrong shape for
the role is a candidate you will spend an hour on
tomorrow, and then an hour the day after that, and
then a slot in your interview loop that you would
rather give to someone else. The discipline of "no,
this is not a fit" is the same discipline as "yes,
this is". Both are work.

By the end of day two you should have 3-6 tracked
candidates and a few sentences of notes on each. The
notes are the most important thing you produce this
week. They are what your team will read in two
weeks when they ask "why did we track this person?",
and they are what you will read when the same
question comes up in your own head.

## Day 3 — save the search, then stop searching

The single highest-leverage thing in the product is
saving the queries from day one. A saved search
becomes a radar, and a radar runs on a schedule and
tells you about the people who appear *after* you
looked. Sourcing is not a snapshot problem; the
candidates you want are the ones you would have
found if you had searched again in a week, plus the
ones you would have missed.

Set the radar frequency. Daily is the default for an
active role; weekly is the default for a passive
role. Pick the destination that matches how you
work: email if you live in your inbox, in-app inbox
if you live in BuilderHunt, both if you want
redundancy.

By the end of day three you should have 1-3 saved
searches running on a schedule, the same 3-6
tracked candidates you had at the end of day two,
and a list of the queries that did not work. The
queries that did not work are not failures; they
are the noise floor of the problem you are solving,
and they tell you what the right queries are by
ruling out the wrong ones.

## Day 4 — write three outreach drafts

Pick the two best tracked candidates. For each one,
use the outreach generator to produce a first draft.
Read the `hookSource` field first. If the source is
`bio` and the bio is a sentence the candidate wrote
in 2018, the personalisation line is honest but
stale. If the source is `topic` and the topic is
"webassembly", the personalisation line is a label,
not a hook. The generator is a first-draft tool;
the editor is you.

Write three drafts: two for the best candidates, one
for a candidate you are not sure about. The
"unsure" candidate is the one that teaches you
whether your queries are well-tuned. A draft that
reads as generic and templated is a signal that
the candidate is in your list for the wrong
reasons; a draft that reads as specific and
relevant is a signal that the query is good.

Do not send the drafts today. The drafts are for
you, to see whether the candidates you are about
to email are the candidates you actually want to
email. Sending them on day four is too early; you
will have more information on day five.

## Day 5 — send the first batch, write the second

Re-read the drafts from yesterday. Two of them
should still feel right. Send those two. Use your
own email account, not the platform. The platform
drafts; the transport is yours.

Now look at your radar inbox. If the radar has been
running for 24 hours, it has probably surfaced one
or two new candidates. Read them. Track the
interesting ones, leave the rest. Add the
candidates to the shortlist for next week's
sending batch.

Write the next two outreach drafts — one for the
strongest new candidate from the radar, one for a
candidate you did not write about on day four. The
pace is: two sends a day, every day, until the role
is filled. The pace is also: do not write more
than two. The cost of writing a third draft is the
opportunity cost of writing a second one tomorrow,
and the second one tomorrow will be informed by
whatever the first two sent today told you.

By the end of day five you should have sent two
emails, have 6-8 tracked candidates, have 1-3 saved
searches running, and have a sense of whether the
queries are returning the right kind of candidate.
The week is not a finish line. The week is the
start of a loop that you run for as long as the
role is open.

## What to do in week two

The loop above, every week, until the role is
filled or you decide to kill it. Two sends a day,
two new candidates tracked per day, one query
refined or retired per day, one saved search
maintained. The cadence is small on purpose; a
sourcing habit that demands an hour a day is a
sourcing habit you will skip on a busy day, and a
sourcing habit you skip is a sourcing habit that
stops working.

A useful weekly review: which of the queries I
wrote last week is producing the best candidates?
Which is producing nothing? Retire the empty
ones, refine the noisy ones, keep the useful
ones. A query that has been running for two weeks
and has produced zero tracked candidates is a
query that should be killed, not a query that
should be given more time.

A useful monthly review: which sources are
returning the best candidates for this kind of
role? A search for a Go specialist that gets
its best results from npm and GitHub is a
search that should not include Reddit. A search
for a frontend generalist that gets its best
results from DEV.to and Hashnode is a search
that should not include Hugging Face. The
source selector is not a "more is better"
control; it is a "pick the right ones" control.

## What this plan is not

It is not a recipe for filling a role in a week.
Some roles take a month, some take three. The
plan above is what the first week of a month
looks like. The second week is the same plan
with more tracked candidates and more
refinement.

It is not a recipe for filling a role without
talking to anyone. The platform is a sourcing
tool. The interview, the reference check, the
offer conversation — those are the rest of
hiring, and BuilderHunt stops at the
conversation step. The interview booking flow
is the last thing the platform does; the rest
is the work you do with the candidate you
booked.

It is not a plan for a team. The plan above is
shaped for one person with one open role. A
team of three sourcing for five roles at the
same time is a different cadence, a different
shortlist shape, and a different share model.
The shared-searches and team-activity-feed
features exist for that case, and they are
[on the roadmap](/roadmap) waiting for the
right moment to ship.

## What the first week will look like if it works

A shortlist of 6-8 candidates you have read
something by, 1-3 saved searches running, 2
emails sent, and a sense of whether the queries
are returning the right kind of candidate. That
is the entire output of a successful first
week. Everything else — the interviews, the
offers, the eventual hire — is downstream of
the shortlist, and the shortlist is downstream
of the searches you saved on day three.

The work is small and the work is daily. The
product is a substrate, not a substitute. The
five-day plan is the smallest version of the
habit that fills the role, and the habit is
the thing that works.

[Start a search](/search) and run the plan.
The free tier covers the loop; the paid tiers
add the AI features that make steps 1 and 2
faster. The plan is the same either way.
