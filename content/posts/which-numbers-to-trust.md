Sourcing tools are in the business of showing you numbers about strangers. Almost
none of them will tell you which of those numbers are measured, which are
derived, and which are decoration.

This is that list for BuilderHunt. It is not flattering in places, which is the
point — a number you cannot trace is worse than no number, because it looks like
evidence.

## Measured

**Recency.** When the source says an account last did something, that is the
source's own timestamp. It is the single most load-bearing input to the score and
it is real for GitHub, GitLab, Codeberg, SourceHut, Reddit, DEV.to, Hashnode,
Lobsters, npm and Hugging Face.

**The match explanation.** "matches postgres, performance in topic" is derived
directly from the query and the fields it hit. If you cannot see why a result is
in your list, the row is telling you something.

**Uptime on [/status](/status).** Computed from per-component health snapshots taken
on an interval, 90 days retained. Not a badge someone typed.

**Your own counts** — tracked builders, saved searches, notes, sprint results,
credits. Those are rows in a database, counted.

## Derived, and labelled as such

**The match score.** A recency-weighted combination of signals, not a probability
of anything. A 61 does not mean "61% likely to be a good hire" — it means this
person ranked above a 55 for this query, on the inputs described above. Treat it
as an ordering, never as a measurement.

**Semantic similarity.** Real cosine distance against a real embedding, shown per
result. It measures topical closeness of public work, which is not the same thing
as skill.

**Project hygiene.** Derived from actual GitHub signals now, with a deterministic
fallback that says "not enough data" rather than guessing. It used to be part
heuristic and part invented, and that was a bug I fixed rather than a feature I
tuned.

**Account risk**, used internally for abuse detection: a decayed combination of
signals, currently in observe mode, never shown as a judgement about a builder.

## Synthesized by a source adapter — do not read these as facts

Here is the uncomfortable part. Several fields the UI renders in a uniform way
are, for specific sources, generated by the adapter rather than fetched:

- **Stack Overflow** sets the `bio` to an accept-rate string, and sets "last seen"
  to the moment of the request — which means every Stack Overflow result harvests
  full recency credit whether or not the person has been active.
- **npm** sets the `bio` to "Maintains &lt;package&gt; on npm", and puts a 0–1 quality
  score into the field the UI labels as a follower count. A number that looks like
  100,000 followers is a 1.0 quality score multiplied out.
- **Hacker News** sets `topics` to your own query keywords — so "matches your
  topic" for an HN result is circular — and sets the `bio` to the title of a post.
- **Lobsters** has no bio at all.
- **GitHub repository** results use the repository description as the bio, because
  a repository is not a person and does not have one.

None of that is dishonesty at the adapter layer — a federated search has to
normalize fifteen differently-shaped APIs into one row type, and a missing field
has to become *something*. It becomes a defect at the presentation layer, where
the same visual treatment implies the same provenance.

I know which fields these are because they are written down in the repository's
own ground-truth document, and they are on the list to fix. Until they are fixed:
treat `bio`, `followersCount`, `topics` and "last seen" as per-source fields, and
click through to the original before you rely on any of them. Every result links
out.

## The rule I try to hold myself to

An audit of my own landing page found social proof that nothing counted and a
confidence figure that measured nothing. Both are deleted, and
[the changelog entry](/changelog/we-removed-trust-claims-we-could-not-prove) says
so, because a deletion nobody announces is indistinguishable from never having
done it.

The rule: if a number cannot be traced to a source, it does not ship. When one
gets through anyway — and one just did, in the fields above — the fix is to name
it publicly, not to wait until someone notices.

If you find a number in BuilderHunt you cannot trace, that is a bug report and I
want it: hello@builderhunt.dev.

## Why this is a feature and not an apology

The reason to care, if you are the one doing the sourcing: every claim in this
product is supposed to be checkable in one click. That is the actual differentiator
against a tool that hands you a ranked list and a confidence percentage and
declines to show its work. An evidence panel on every result — showing exactly
which posts and commits moved the score — is the next step, and it is
[on the roadmap](/roadmap) waiting for votes.

[Run a search](/explore?q=postgres) and click through a result to its source. If
the evidence does not hold up, that is the useful outcome.
