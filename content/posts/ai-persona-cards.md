You open a builder's profile. There's a card at the top: two sentences on what they
focus on, an estimated seniority, three to six short strengths, a line on coding
style. None of it was on the bio. All of it was generated by a model the moment you
opened the page.

That card is useful, and it is dangerous, and this post is about the difference
between those two things so you can use it without getting burned.

## What the card actually is

It is a single AI task called `profile-enrich`, registered in the shared task
registry, gated by the AI platform's credit budget, cached for 30 days, and stored
on the per-organization tracked-builder row rather than on a global builder record.
The output schema is small on purpose:

```ts
export const builderAIEnrichmentModelSchema = z.object({
  summary: z.string().min(20).max(400),
  estimatedSeniority: z.enum(['junior', 'mid', 'senior', 'lead']),
  primaryFocus: z.string().min(3).max(120),
  strengths: z.array(z.string().min(2).max(40)).min(1).max(6),
  codingStyle: z.string().min(3).max(200),
})
```

Five fields. Every one of them validated before render, with strict length bounds
and an enum on seniority. The schema is a contract: the model cannot make a
"junior/mid/senior/lead" judgement come out as a number, and it cannot write a
300-word essay. If the output does not parse, the card shows "not enough public
activity for an AI summary" — not a fallback hallucination.

The input is also narrow. The task receives the builder's username, display name,
source, public bio, topics, language, country, follower count, and a list of
public-work highlights. It does **not** receive notes, your saved searches, the
team's tracked builders, the candidate's contact information, or anything from
any other organization. The privacy line is hard: the input is the same row any
visitor could see, plus the highlight list, and nothing else.

## What triggers it, and what doesn't

Three triggers, in this order:

1. **You open a profile detail page for a builder your organization tracks.** A
   job fires, the result is persisted to your org's metadata, and the card
   renders on the next request.
2. **The builder claims and verifies their own profile.** A fresh enrichment runs
   so the card reflects the work they want shown, not the work the indexer saw
   first. The verify path is bound to the source identity it came from — proving
   you own a GitHub account proves *that*, not identity in general.
3. **A row owner or a platform admin hits "refresh".** That path is logged, the
   credit cost is real, and the new version replaces the old one with the schema
   version bumped.

What does not trigger it: opening a builder you have not tracked, a search-result
list, a saved search preview, anything on a public radar, or anything in a
digest. The card is detail-only. The cost reason is honest: enrichment on a
list view would burn credits on profiles you never open, and the cache would
fill with cards nobody reads.

## What the card deliberately will not say

The schema does not include a "verdict" field, a numeric "fit score", or a
recommendation. The domain contract for `profile-enrich` lists them as
prohibited outputs the same way some contracts list prohibited function calls
— they are not an omission, they are an explicit rule the model is told to
break output if it tries. The same rule lives in the
[work-sample analysis task](/changelog/work-sample-analysis) and the
[team-fit analysis task](/changelog/team-fit-analysis). Three separate tasks
with the same prohibition, by design.

A model that has read a few public repositories and a list of topics is not
qualified to say "this person is senior", and it is not qualified to say
"this person is not senior". What it can do is summarise what their public
work is *about* — and the seniority bucket is a label of where their public
work sits in the experience distribution, not a prediction of their level.
Two equally competent engineers can produce cards that look very different
because one of them does not publish, and that is a feature, not a bug.

## What to do with the card, then

Treat it as a reading aid. If the card says "primary focus: distributed
consensus protocols, strengths: formal verification, low-level systems", and
the row's actual public work is a Haskell library for Raft, the card is doing
its job. If the card says that and the public work is a Rails tutorial repo
from 2019, the card is wrong, and a refresh is the appropriate response —
either the indexer has stale data, or the model hallucinated, and either is
information you want to know.

It is not a substitute for reading the repos. It is not a screening filter.
It is the difference between opening a profile and seeing nothing, and
opening a profile and seeing a one-paragraph orientation. The rest of the
job is yours.

## The two real failure modes

The honest disclosure, because a card that fails silently is worse than no
card:

**A card that fills in detail the builder never showed.** A profile with an
empty bio and three repos still gets a card, because the highlights list and
the topics give the model something to anchor on. The card will be shorter
and vaguer than a card generated from a rich public profile, but it will
exist. The model is told to say "not enough public activity" if the input is
genuinely empty, and the schema enforces a 20-character minimum on the
summary so a one-word response fails validation.

**A card that goes stale.** A 30-day cache is long. A builder can ship
something significant in 30 days, and the card will keep describing the
version of them that existed when you first opened the profile. The "refresh"
action is the answer, and the credit cost is honest: refreshes go through
the same registry as the original, so the platform can tell you what they
cost and can stop them when the budget is gone.

## What this looks like in the app

![A builder profile with the AI Persona card at the top showing summary, seniority, focus and strengths](/images/blog/persona-card.webp)

*The card sits at the top of the profile detail view, with the persona
fields above the per-source activity, code-style fingerprint and team-fit
analysis. Each of those is its own panel and its own task, fed by the
same tracked-builder row but not by each other.*

## When to skip it

If the builder has fewer than about 50 followers across all sources, an empty
bio, fewer than three public repos, and no posts, the card is more likely to
be wrong than useful. The platform will still render it if it exists; you
are the one who can decide "this card is filler, I want to read the repos
myself instead". The right reflex there is not to delete the card — it
costs you nothing to render — but to be appropriately skeptical of it.

[Try it on a real profile](/search) — search a name, open the detail view,
and decide for yourself whether the card speeds you up or slows you down.
That is the test the feature is meant to pass, and the only one that matters.
